# The Daily: Discovery Has to Come Before Extraction

A contract agent cannot reliably extract what the team has not yet defined. Before building the checklist, I needed to understand how reviewers actually move a contract through intake, subject-matter review, status updates, and signature.

Today I documented a contract review workflow and used the discussion to narrow an AI proof of concept. The work exposed the difference between automating document reading and automating a real operating process.

## The theme: Map the judgment before designing the agent

The workflow started with notifications and a work-in-progress queue. A reviewer monitored new and active contracts, checked whether other specialists needed to participate, updated ownership and status, and then moved into document review.

The initial checks were concrete. Reviewers verified the legal entity name, required commercial terms, billing and shipping information, signature blocks, and whether additional approval was needed. Those checks are good candidates for structured assistance because they can be expressed as observable evidence.

Other parts were less deterministic. Contract type, urgency, unusual attributes, and exceptions changed who needed to review the document and how quickly it could proceed. Those decisions depend on context and should not be hidden behind a single confidence score.

## Field notes from the build

The working session deliberately focused on pain points and mentally expensive work. Waiting for specialist input, tracking status across systems, handling contract variability, and determining what belonged in an initial checklist were all discussed.

This helped narrow the proof of concept. The first version should not attempt to replace the entire contract lifecycle. It should identify a small set of required fields and document checks, surface missing or inconsistent information, and leave exception handling with the reviewer.

The conversation also highlighted the risk of encoding the current workflow too literally. Existing steps may reflect system limitations rather than an ideal future state. Discovery needs to capture what happens today, why it happens, and which controls must survive if the workflow changes.

Human oversight was explicitly part of the scope discussion. The group needed to identify where review was mandatory and which exception types should be excluded from the first proof of concept. That is a better design posture than adding a generic disclaimer after the agent is built.

Licensing and platform dependencies were also raised. A prototype that depends on unavailable capabilities is not a viable workflow. Architecture decisions need to include the access model and operating cost from the beginning.

A steering document from the same day also recorded approval to disable federated Copilot connectors by default and enable them case by case. I am carrying that only as supporting context. It reinforces a common pattern: start narrow, review the data boundary, and expand intentionally.

## What moved

-

Documented the current contract intake and review workflow.

-

Identified concrete document checks that could support an initial agent-assisted checklist.

-

Distinguished deterministic checks from contextual review and exception handling.

-

Captured workflow pain points involving specialist coordination, status tracking, and contract variability.

-

Defined human oversight and out-of-scope exceptions as design requirements for the proof of concept.

-

Incorporated licensing and platform dependencies into the discovery discussion.

## The reusable pattern

Start AI discovery with the work, not the model.

Map triggers, queues, handoffs, evidence, decisions, exceptions, and completion states. For each step, ask whether it is deterministic, judgment-based, or merely compensating for a system limitation.

Automate the deterministic checks first. Present evidence for reviewer verification. Route exceptions instead of forcing a prediction. Preserve status and ownership as structured data.

The future state is a workflow-aware agent that can extract required fields, compare them with an approved checklist, identify missing evidence, and update a governed work item without becoming the final legal authority.

## Standing takeaways

-

Extraction quality depends on a clearly defined checklist.

-

Current-state discovery should capture why a step exists, not only that it exists.

-

Exception handling belongs in the architecture from the beginning.

-

Human oversight should be tied to specific decisions and risk conditions.

-

Platform access and licensing are part of solution feasibility.

## Open thread

The next step is to convert the workflow into an MVP checklist with required evidence, excluded exception types, reviewer checkpoints, and representative contracts for evaluation.
